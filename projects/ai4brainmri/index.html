Comparative Study of Anatomical and Learned Features in AI Models for Structural Brain MRI

Abstract
We compare three approaches to extracting features from structural brain MRI: (1) computation of anatomical surfaces and volumes, (2) supervised learning with convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and (3) unsupervised pretraining of vision transformers (ViTs), followed by supervised finetuning. Our study is based on 18 publicly available datasets containing 3D structural T1-weighted MRI scans from approximately 80,000 participants across seven clinical tasks. A linear model using anatomical features achieves diagnostic performance comparable to CNNs and pretrained ViTs, including models pretrained on thousands of scans. We also find that CNNs and pretrained ViTs capture anatomical information without explicit feature extraction. We introduce Anatomy Segmentation Pretraining (ASP) to incorporate this information during ViT pretraining. ASP improves biological age estimation over the other models evaluated in this study.
Study Overview

Three ways to extract features from a brain MRI

The study uses 18 public datasets for pretraining and evaluation across seven clinical tasks. We compare linear models using anatomical measurements, CNNs trained from scratch, and pretrained vision transformers. For the transformers, we evaluate both masked autoencoding alone and additional Anatomy Segmentation Pretraining (ASP).

Anatomical

Linear model on FreeSurfer features

Logistic or linear regression on 178 regional volumes, cortical surface areas, and cortical thickness values extracted with FreeSurfer's recon-all pipeline.

CNN

Supervised ResNet / U-Net

ResNet-18 for classification tasks and U-Net for segmentation, trained end-to-end from scratch on each task's labeled data.

Pretrained ViT

ViT with masked autoencoding

A 3D ViT-Base pretrained via masked autoencoding (MAE) on 69,878 MRI scans, then adapted per task with linear probing, LoRA, or full finetuning.

ASP ViT

Anatomy Segmentation Pretraining

An MAE-pretrained ViT further trained to segment brain regions, so that its image embedding encodes anatomical information.

Method

Anatomy Segmentation Pretraining (ASP)

The performance of anatomical-feature models motivates using anatomy as a pretraining target. ASP builds on masked autoencoding by training the ViT to predict brain-region segmentation masks from its image embedding.

1

Start from MAE pretraining. A 3D ViT-Base is first pretrained with masked autoencoding on the full pretraining cohort. Linear probing shows that the embedding becomes more predictive of brain volumes and demographics during pretraining, before those gains level off.

2

Decode from the image embedding. The mask decoder has no skip connections from the encoder. It therefore relies on the global ViT embedding for anatomical information, without access to local encoder features as in a U-Net.

3

Segment one region at a time. To reduce the memory needed for labels, one of 39 regions is sampled at each training step. Its learned embedding is added to the image embedding, and the decoder predicts a binary mask for that region. This approach is inspired by prompt-based segmentation models such as SAM.

4

Freeze, then alternate. The encoder is first frozen while the decoder learns to read out existing MAE features. Training then alternates between segmentation and MAE epochs, to help retain the visual features learned during initial pretraining.

The 39 regions were selected from an initial FreeSurfer parcellation of 112 regions by keeping disease-relevant structures and merging symmetric or anatomically related regions, discarding only those contributing under 1% of total brain volume.

Results

Diagnosis and lesion segmentation

We evaluate Alzheimer's diagnosis across eight cohorts, Parkinson's diagnosis, epilepsy diagnosis, tumor IDH-mutation detection, whole-tumor segmentation, and stroke-lesion segmentation. Most comparisons do not show statistically significant differences between models. Anatomical logistic regression remains competitive on diagnostic tasks; CNNs and ViTs are also compared on segmentation.

Brain age estimation in a larger cohort

For biological age estimation, the cohort is substantially larger (n≈62,890). Here, ASP ViT has the lowest prediction error among the models evaluated, both internally and on two independent external cohorts.

Discussion

Discussion and implications

Anatomical features remain competitive. Across public cohorts of a few hundred to a few thousand scans, a linear model on FreeSurfer features performs comparably to CNNs and pretrained ViTs. The regression model is inexpensive to train and its features are interpretable.

Dataset size may limit diagnostic performance. The three approaches achieve similar results on the public datasets studied. This suggests that larger cohorts could be useful, though these comparisons alone do not establish what limits performance.

Segmentation supervision helps ViTs learn anatomy. Anatomical information develops gradually during MAE pretraining. ASP accelerates this process and improves prediction on the larger brain-age estimation task.

Faster segmentation could reduce feature-extraction costs. Although linear models are inexpensive to train, extracting anatomical features can be costly. Tools such as FastSurfer could reduce this cost and make anatomical baselines easier to apply to larger cohorts.
