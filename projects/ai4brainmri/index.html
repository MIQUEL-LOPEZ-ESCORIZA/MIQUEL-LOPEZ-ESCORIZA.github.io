Abstract
We compare three approaches to extracting features from structural brain MRI: (1) computation of anatomical surfaces and volumes, (2) supervised learning with convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and (3) unsupervised pretraining of vision transformers (ViTs), followed by supervised finetuning. Our study is based on 18 publicly available datasets containing 3D structural T1-weighted MRI scans from approximately 80,000 participants across seven clinical tasks. A linear model using anatomical features achieves diagnostic performance comparable to CNNs and pretrained ViTs, including models pretrained on thousands of scans. We also find that CNNs and pretrained ViTs capture anatomical information without explicit feature extraction. We introduce Anatomy Segmentation Pretraining (ASP) to incorporate this information during ViT pretraining. ASP improves biological age estimation over the other models evaluated in this study.
Study Overview

Three ways to extract features from a brain MRI

The study uses 18 public datasets for pretraining and evaluation across seven clinical tasks. We compare linear models using anatomical measurements, CNNs trained from scratch, and pretrained vision transformers. For the transformers, we evaluate both masked autoencoding alone and additional Anatomy Segmentation Pretraining (ASP).

Anatomical

Linear model on FreeSurfer features

Logistic or linear regression on 178 regional volumes, cortical surface areas, and cortical thickness values extracted with FreeSurfer's recon-all pipeline.

CNN

Supervised ResNet / U-Net

ResNet-18 for classification tasks and U-Net for segmentation, trained end-to-end from scratch on each task's labeled data.

Pretrained ViT

ViT with masked autoencoding

A 3D ViT-Base trained to reconstruct masked MRI patches. The source cohort contains 69,878 scans from 65,249 participants, with a participant-level holdout excluded from optimization.

ASP ViT

Anatomy Segmentation Pretraining

An MAE-pretrained ViT further trained to segment brain regions, so that its image embedding encodes anatomical information.

Cohorts and evaluation

The pretraining data come from UK Biobank, HCP, CamCAN, and IXI. All splits are made at the participant level, keeping repeated scans together. Ten percent of UK Biobank participants (6,950 scans) are excluded from MAE and ASP optimization. This holdout is used to monitor anatomical and demographic probes during pretraining and later to evaluate age estimation internally.

For Alzheimer's diagnosis, models are trained on ADNI and tested on held-out ADNI participants and seven independent cohorts: NACC, AIBL, MIRIAD, and OASIS 1–4. The study also compares BrainIAC, a separately pretrained model, and UNETR for lesion segmentation. BrainIAC is excluded from the Alzheimer's comparison because its pretraining data overlap with several external test cohorts.

Method

Anatomy Segmentation Pretraining (ASP)

The performance of anatomical-feature models motivates using anatomy as a pretraining target. ASP builds on masked autoencoding by training the ViT to predict brain-region segmentation masks from its image embedding.

1

Start from MAE pretraining. A 3D ViT-Base reconstructs MRI volumes with 75% of their 16 × 16 × 16-voxel patches masked. This first stage runs for 1,000 epochs, learning an image representation without diagnostic labels.

2

Decode from the image embedding. The decoder takes a 768-dimensional image embedding and upsamples it to a 128 × 128 × 128 mask through six 3D transposed-convolution blocks. With no skip connections, the decoder must obtain anatomical information from this embedding.

3

Segment one region at a time. To reduce the memory needed for labels, one of 39 regions is sampled at each training step. Its learned embedding is added to the image embedding, and the decoder predicts a binary mask for that region. This approach is inspired by prompt-based segmentation models such as SAM.

4

Freeze, then alternate. The encoder is frozen for 100 epochs while the decoder learns to use the MAE features. Encoder and decoder are then trained jointly for 400 epochs, alternating segmentation and masked reconstruction to help retain the features learned during MAE pretraining.

The segmentation targets are generated by FreeSurfer, starting from 112 anatomical labels. Symmetric and related structures are merged into 39 regions. Small regions are excluded when they also lack a strong disease association; disease-relevant structures such as the hippocampus are retained. These are automated anatomical labels, distinct from the manual tumor and stroke masks used for downstream evaluation.

Adapting the encoder to clinical tasks

Parkinson's diagnosis and IDH-mutation prediction use linear probes on frozen ViT features. The other downstream tasks use low-rank adaptation (LoRA), which trains small updates to the attention layers together with the task head. Full finetuning is evaluated separately in the ablations. For tumor and stroke segmentation, the encoder is paired with a U-Net–style decoder; this differs from the bottleneck decoder used during ASP.

Except for the already standardized lesion-segmentation cohorts, scans undergo FreeSurfer preprocessing and quality control. Images and masks are aligned to MNI152 space at 1 mm isotropic resolution. ASP therefore uses anatomical supervision during pretraining, even though a downstream prediction does not require the 178 extracted anatomical measurements as inputs.

Results

Diagnosis and lesion segmentation

We evaluate Alzheimer's diagnosis across eight cohorts, Parkinson's diagnosis, epilepsy diagnosis, tumor IDH-mutation detection, whole-tumor segmentation, and stroke-lesion segmentation. Most comparisons do not show statistically significant differences between models. Anatomical logistic regression remains competitive on diagnostic tasks; CNNs and ViTs are also compared on segmentation.

The Alzheimer's task distinguishes healthy controls, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and Alzheimer's disease. Training uses soft labels of 0, 0.5, and 1; evaluation includes pairwise AUCs and their macro-average. ASP has the highest macro-AUC in seven of eight cohorts, but the paper reports a significant improvement over the CNN and anatomical model only in OASIS 2.

The MAE-pretrained ViT has the highest AUC for Parkinson’s and epilepsy, while ASP has the highest AUC for tumor IDH-mutation prediction. The IDH differences are not statistically significant.

With all training data, U-Net has the highest tumor Dice score and the MAE-pretrained ViT has the highest stroke Dice score. The paper reports significant differences only against UNETR in these comparisons. When tumor training data are reduced to 10%, both pretrained ViTs outperform U-Net and UNETR.

The epilepsy experiment has only 82 training scans, and no CNN result is reported because of the limited training data. EPISURG contains only epilepsy cases: its probability distribution shows how the model scores those cases, but cannot establish specificity or an external case–control AUC.

Brain age estimation in a larger cohort

Age estimation uses approximately 62,890 training scans from UK Biobank, HCP, CamCAN, and IXI. Evaluation uses 6,950 held-out UK Biobank scans and healthy-control scans from NACC (1,136) and ADNI (2,791). ASP ViT has the lowest mean absolute error in all three cohorts. Here, brain age is the age predicted from MRI, with chronological age used as the regression target.

ASP's advantage over MAE pretraining alone is 0.35 years internally and more than a year on each external cohort. The age breakdowns show larger model differences above 80, an age range less represented in training. Predicted ages tend to be higher for dementia patients than for healthy controls in the same age bins, with a smaller shift for MCI. These group differences should be read alongside the age-dependent prediction bias visible in the plots.

Discussion

Discussion and implications

Anatomical features remain competitive. Across public cohorts of a few hundred to a few thousand scans, a linear model on FreeSurfer features performs comparably to CNNs and pretrained ViTs. The regression model is inexpensive to train and its features are interpretable.

Dataset size may limit diagnostic performance. The three approaches achieve similar results on the public datasets studied. This suggests that larger cohorts could be useful, though these comparisons alone do not establish what limits performance.

Segmentation supervision helps ViTs learn anatomy. Anatomical information develops gradually during MAE pretraining. ASP accelerates this process and improves prediction on the larger brain-age estimation task.

Faster segmentation could reduce feature-extraction costs. Although linear models are inexpensive to train, FreeSurfer preprocessing averaged 6.11 hours per scan in the study’s analysis of 1,500 successful processing logs. Tools such as FastSurfer could reduce this cost; that proposed substitution is not evaluated here.

These findings concern the structural T1-weighted MRI tasks and cohorts studied. Similar diagnostic scores do not establish that the models are equivalent. Because the internal age holdout was also used for pretraining probes, the independent NACC and ADNI evaluations provide an important check on generalization.
